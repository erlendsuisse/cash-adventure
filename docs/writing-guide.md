# Cash Adventure writing guide

How every card, choice and outcome should read. The game is read on screen
**and aloud** by the narrator, to players aged 10-13 and adults, so the text
must be quick to take in by eye and by ear.

## The voice

- **"You", present tense.** The player is the merchant: "You step off the boat."
- **A warm storyteller with a wink.** Gentle humour, never sarcasm. The city is
  a lively storybook harbour with Victorian steampunk touches.
- **Vessarin is a real place.** Use its sounds, smells and sights: gulls, salt
  wind, lanterns, cobbles, bells, steam, the counting-houses, the docks.
- **People have voices.** Most cards have one character who speaks, with one
  telling detail ("a dockhand with tar on her sleeves"). Use their words to
  show who they are, rather than describing them.

## Shape of a card

1. **Hook first.** The first sentence makes something happen or puts a person
   in front of you. Never open with background or explanation.
2. **Stakes in the middle.** What could you win, and what could go wrong?
3. **Land on the decision.** End where the player has to choose.

Aim for **2-4 short sentences, about 20-45 words** in the body. Chapter openers
and Colossus trials may run a little longer.

## Sentences for the ear

- Mostly **under 15 words**; never over 20. Mix short and medium for rhythm.
- **Concrete nouns and active verbs**: "The crate splits open" rather than "The
  goods were damaged."
- **Land the important word at the end** of the sentence.
- Plain words. If a 10-year-old would stumble over it aloud, pick another.
- Numbers as digits (50 gold, 3 days); the UI makes them bold.
- No modern words: the Watch, not police; coins and ledgers, not money transfers.

## Choices

- **Start with a verb**, keep it short, make the intent clear: "Buy the
  stall", "Haggle him down", "Walk away".
- Keep the mechanics in brackets exactly as the game needs them:
  `(70g, +21g/month)`, `(Savvy check, DC 12)`, `(-90g)`.
- Saying no should still have character: "Keep your coins", "Wave her off",
  not just "Decline".

## Outcomes

- **One or two sentences** that show what happened and how it feels.
- **Something specific**: "Steam curls from your kiosks all night" rather than
  "Profits follow."
- A refusal still gets a small, human ending: "She shrugs and tries the next
  stall down."

## Tone guardrails (kids and adults)

- Danger is real but never gruesome. Threats, scams, storms and rivals are
  fine; blood, killing, drugs and adult themes are not.
- Crime is shown as shady deals, smuggling and trickery, without glorifying it.
- Setbacks sting but stay hopeful. There is always a next move.

## Before and after

> **Before:** A cosmic force sweeps through space-time. The old economy is
> dead. Something new emerges from the chaos.
>
> **After:** The sky over Vessarin turns violet. Every scale in the market
> swings at once, and the old prices simply melt away. Whoever moves first
> will write the new ones.

Sources behind these rules: writing for the ear (short, conversational
sentences that land on clear nouns), middle-grade writing (hooks, active
verbs, sentences under 20 words, never talking down), and choice-driven card
games like Reigns (short, punchy text where the player can guess the outcome
of a choice before making it).
